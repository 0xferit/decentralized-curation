### R09. External manipulation benefits need comparison with the security budget

**Priority: important research obligation. Status: conditional attack model.** Locations: slides 7, 8, 17 and 19.

A beneficiary, competitor or briber can value the change in funding more than curator rewards. If captured funding or external benefit over time exceeds forfeited stake, prediction-bet loss, fees and capital cost, a deviation can remain profitable. Continuing accrual makes the time horizon important. A losing appeal bet does not itself reverse the adopted score.

**Recommendation:** Evaluate net coalition wealth against a stated nonmanipulative baseline over a common time horizon. Include actual acquisition and financing costs, recoverable principal, token-value changes, net appeal transfers and external gains. The conditional captured-jury example does not establish that obtaining control is affordable or profitable.

**User-proposed capture defenses:** These are economically relevant and must be analyzed jointly, rather than treating a coherent coalition’s zero distance-slashing as zero capture cost.

1. **Acquisition friction.** An application-specific utility token can make acquiring substantial eligible voting weight difficult when sell-side inventory is limited. “Exponentially expensive” is not established by shallow liquidity alone. For a fixed constant-product market, ignoring fees, buying a fraction f of its token reserve costs Yf/(1-f), where Y is its initial quote-asset reserve: 50%, 75%, 90% and 99% cost Y, 3Y, 9Y and 99Y. This is a hyperbolic relation for one market, not a token-wide supply curve. Existing holders, other venues and available borrowing or bribery paths need separate treatment. Total token supply, eligible pool stake and a realized jury’s weight are different quantities. Source: [Uniswap v2 whitepaper](https://app.uniswap.org/whitepaper.pdf).
2. **Loss of future token value.** Jury work can support demand for the participation token. If a detected capture causes meaningful loss of demand, a controller can destroy the value of its own remaining holdings even without protocol slashing. This is strongest when the controller retains substantial exposure and users can identify and leave unreliable service. It does not establish that the loss exceeds external capture gains, or that separately deciding holders will refuse bribes when no individual can prevent the collapse. The latter distinction appears in [Karakostas, Kiayias and Zacharias, section 7.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.06352v2); their blockchain model is not a theorem about this design.
3. **Community fork.** Any group may coordinate replacement contracts and a replacement utility token using its own exclusion list, without proving misconduct or obtaining permission. This can remove suspected attackers' participation rights on that replacement and reduce the value of their old holdings if actual use and funding migrate. It is a social recovery possibility, not an automatic protocol penalty. Exclusion is the proposing group's judgment, not a protocol finding of misconduct; innocent holders can be excluded and attackers can remain included. Users and funders decide voluntarily which fork to adopt. A token fork does not by itself recover ether already paid or transfer balances from old immutable contracts. Sources: [Kleros’s fork-friendly design proposal](https://blog.kleros.io/towards-kleros-v2/) and [Buterin’s subjectivity analysis](https://blog.ethereum.org/2015/02/14/subjectivity-exploitability-tradeoff).

**Joint accounting:** Acquisition expense and later destruction of the purchased asset’s value must not be counted twice. For an outsider paying 100 and ultimately recovering 20, the asset loss is 80 before other costs. Token depreciation and migration to a fork can be two causes of the same loss, not independent penalties to add. The fork’s deterrent effect depends on whether users and funders identify a preferred replacement and actually adopt it. Removing a formal proof requirement does not establish that the selected exclusions identify the attacking coalition. [Augur’s security model](https://www.augur.net/whitepapers/augur-v2/incentives-and-security/) explicitly conditions its security claim on migration and the value of tokens in the losing fork; it does not validate this mechanism.

**Recovery design:** The accepted decisions and the implementation defaults selected under delegated judgment are consolidated in the [voluntary fork recovery design](fork-recovery-design.md). That document is authoritative for the recovery procedure. It covers the pre-incident snapshot, declared exclusions, replacement claims, fresh assessments, individual funder migration, proportional accounting, protected obligations and conditional funding. The common service budget remains separate from contributors' migration entitlements.

**Status:** The recovery procedure is specified as a working design; deployment, bounded-cost accounting, custody adapters and an end-to-end rehearsal are not implemented or verified here. Its economic effectiveness remains conditional on adoption, reliable operation and the attacker's costs and outside gains. A reproducible package does not validate accusations, recover paid ETH or prove that an excluded attacker cannot re-enter.

### R10. Overlapping nominations can multiply weight unless the admission policy prevents it

**Priority: important research obligation. Status: workshop policy hardened; semantic attribution remains a human assessment.** Locations: slides 3, 5 and 8.

Against other active weight 0.9, one 0.6 nomination receives 40% of the flow. Two controlled entries each credited 0.6 receive 57.14% combined. The pool prevents concurrent nomination of the same registry entry, but this check does not enforce uniqueness by beneficiary or by underlying work. Distinct entries require their own deposit and fee, and whether they can be admitted depends on the registry and policy.

**Evidence:** [Entry, beneficiary and existing-nomination checks](https://github.com/golemfoundation/octant-v3-monorepo/blob/ca8c8560119dee34f809a31e5149f4a6217467fb/packages/contracts/src/Pool.sol#L701-L709); [deposit and fee collection](https://github.com/golemfoundation/octant-v3-monorepo/blob/ca8c8560119dee34f809a31e5149f4a6217467fb/packages/contracts/src/Pool.sol#L713-L725).

**Recommendation:** Identify the registry's actual overlap controls and add an attribution rule to the worked policy. Compare merged and split descriptions of one contribution. This is not a demonstrated bypass of registry admission.

**Resolution:** The shared workshop policy now requires distinct contribution attribution and prevents independently assigning full credit to duplicate descriptions of the same work. It permits different contributions by one beneficiary. The live policy, deck, handout and public summary consume that one rule. The question notes retain the split-weight counterexample and explain that registry identity, deposits and normalization do not enforce semantic uniqueness. This is a scoped policy defense, not a demonstrated elimination of strategic overlap.

### R11. The larger appeal example has fewer distinct report sources

**Priority: moderate. Status: presentation corrected.** Locations: slides 6–7; [example data](../public/deck/index.html#L86).

The first jury has six tickets and three curator records. The appeal has twelve tickets but two report records, with eight and four tickets. The example demonstrates a larger stake sample and score replacement, not more independent information. The deck correctly warns that seats need not mean independent people, but only the first stage prominently counts curators.

**Recommendation:** Label both stages with ticket count and distinct report sources. Do not describe the change as an improvement in information without an additional independence or competence argument.

**Resolution:** Both the initial and appeal stages now display derived ticket counts and distinct report-source counts. The larger-jury example is explicitly distinguished from additional independent observations. The underlying hypothetical reports and calculations are unchanged.

## Scientific interpretation and demonstration design

### R12. Announcing the expected dispersion can help create it

**Priority: important if observations are interpreted as evidence. Status: advance cue removed; interpretation bounded.** Locations: slide 15; [facilitator prediction](facilitator-guide.md#L79); [question notes](likely-questions.md#L199).

The facilitator announces in advance that the Portable Document Format (PDF) round should have a wider band than Inkscape and Linux. Participants therefore know the desired group pattern. In addition, bounded scores mechanically constrain possible dispersion near the endpoints, so wider bands need not indicate greater substantive disagreement.

**Recommendation:** Record the prediction privately before the session and reveal it afterward. Interpret dispersion alongside its mean and participants' stated reasons. If the cue is retained for teaching, identify the exercise as primed rather than an independent test of the expectation.

**Resolution:** The guide keeps directional forecasts in a private pre-session note until all compared rounds close, then discloses the original prediction including failure. The deck, guide and evaluation plan interpret dispersion with the mean, bounded scale, participation and reasons. The workshop remains an illustrative, interacting sequence, not an independent experiment.

### R13. Assigned manipulation cannot show that incentives caused participants to manipulate

**Priority: important interpretation limit. Status: wording and inference corrected.** Locations: slide 16; [private instruction](../public/r/index.html#L1198).

The application explicitly tells selected people to score 0.10 and supplies a cover story. Compliance can reflect role-play or obedience to the presenter. It is not evidence that participants independently chose collusion because it paid. Earlier rounds concern different nominees and are not an untreated arXiv comparison; jurors also affect one another's payoffs, so they are not independent replications of the whole mechanism. Calling others “honest jurors” additionally attributes a motive not observed from their scores.

**Recommendation:** Say “assigned adversarial role” and “other jurors.” Restrict this session's conclusions to submitted reports and resulting payoffs. A later incentive experiment needs an appropriate comparison, independently chosen strategies and analysis that accounts for interactions between participants. The deck's existing no-safe-threshold warning is correct.

**Resolution:** The joining notice discloses possible assigned adversarial roles and the option to decline. Instructions and explanation use assigned-role and other-juror language rather than inferring honesty from a score. The deck and evaluation plan separate scripted responses and calculated payoffs from voluntary choices; a separate comparison is required for causal incentive claims.

### R14. The bloc slider combines coalition size with a selected membership path

**Priority: moderate. Status: membership path disclosed.** Location: slide 16; [sorted baseline](../public/deck/index.html#L37); [membership rule](../public/deck/index.html#L190).

Increasing bloc share always converts the lowest baseline scores first to 0.90. At three of fifteen seats, replacing the lowest three yields mean 0.550667; replacing the highest three yields 0.520000. Both blocs lose in this particular case. The point is composition sensitivity, not proof that the current path always maximizes attack payoff.

**Recommendation:** State the membership rule, or provide several fixed or seeded assignments. Do not let a smooth slider suggest that stake share alone determines the outcome. The existing rejection of a universal threshold should remain.

**Resolution:** The visible bloc legend states that fixed membership follows the lowest starting scores first, even after a score is edited. The deck and facilitator notes explicitly deny that share alone determines the result. The arithmetic and selected membership path remain unchanged; no claim is made that this path is representative or worst-case.

### R15. The evidence roadmap needs measurements and failure criteria

**Priority: important research obligation. Status: proposed evaluation plan added; empirical validation remains outstanding.** Locations: slides 18–20; [roadmap](../public/deck/index.html#L21).

“Comprehension,” “score stability,” “useful work” and “public benefit” remain different, unoperationalized outcomes. Stable misunderstanding can look reliable; consensus can conceal shared bias. The slides propose comparison with alternatives but specify no primary outcome, affected population, horizon or result that would lower confidence. The complete private Arrow plan may contain details absent here.

**Recommendation:** Add a small decision table naming each construct, its independent measure, comparison and redesign condition. Separate policy-comprehension cases from repeated unchanged-evidence ratings and changed-evidence updating. For public benefit, choose a bounded allocation decision, account for process costs and specify who sets the success criteria. Do not treat this teaching workshop as an efficacy trial.

One useful adversarial example already exists in the actual workshop ledger: with five equal stakes and reports `[0.3, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7]`, the last juror's net is −331.05 points. Changing only that report to `0.5` gives +3,958.54, an improvement of 4,289.59 points; the mean moves from 0.50 to 0.46. If 0.7 represents that person's well-founded assessment and the other reports are anticipated, conformity pays. This is an existence example under stated beliefs, not proof of actual dishonesty or a result for the different contract grading rule. It supports measuring evidence quality separately from payouts.

**Resolution:** The after-demonstration roadmap now names constructs, independent measures and comparisons, and redesign conditions. The [evaluation plan](evaluation-plan.md) specifies separate comprehension, unchanged- and changed-evidence, voluntary-incentive, correction-cost and public-benefit studies; it addresses group dependence and independent-review limitations. Practical success margins and sample sizes must be justified for each study rather than invented for this workshop. This is a proposed plan, not completed evidence or the private Arrow protocol.

## Delivery, accessibility and scrutiny

### R16. The live score slider lacks an accessible name and normalized value description

**Priority: high for affected participants. Status: native scale and accessible semantics corrected; phone assistive-device verification remains.** Locations: slide 15's live task; [range control](../public/r/index.html#L1225).

The range input has no associated label, accessible label attribute or labelled-by reference. Its values run from 0 to 100 while the visible rubric uses 0.00 to 1.00; it supplies no accessible value text explaining that conversion. Native keyboard behavior does not resolve the missing task name and scale explanation. The deck's own sliders have better semantics.

**Recommendation:** Add a meaningful accessible name and normalized value text, then test the entire timed flow with a phone screen reader. Consider a numeric entry alternative. See the [World Wide Web Consortium slider guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/slider/).

**Resolution:** The live range now uses native values from zero to one, a nominee-specific label, normalized value text and keyboard guidance. Input converts to the existing integer representation before sealing. A real-browser regression checks the accessibility tree, Arrow/Home/End behavior, neutral recusal and seal/reveal. Desktop browser semantics do not establish correct behavior with a phone screen reader; that device check remains explicit.

### R17. Important qualifications are much smaller than the claims they qualify

**Priority: moderate delivery risk. Status: typography and responsive layout improved; venue legibility remains untested.** Locations: slides 7, 13, 14, 16 and 22; [deck styles](../public/deck/deck.css#L2).

At a 1280 × 720 viewport, source/qualification text is 11.52 pixels; widget legends on slides 14 and 16 are 10.72 pixels. The shared-policy anchor descriptions are also small. The deck fits the tested viewport, but key limits such as omitted appeal payoffs and differing grading rules are visually subordinate. A caveat present in source may not reach someone at the back of a room.

**Recommendation:** Promote indispensable qualifications into readable body text and speak them explicitly. Rehearse from the rear of the actual room. We found no horizontal overflow at the tested 390-pixel mobile width, so this is a projection and emphasis concern, not a reproduced clipping defect.

**Resolution:** Material limits are promoted into body text and supporting notes and chart keys have a fourteen-pixel floor in the tested desktop viewport. The policy slide retains its five anchors; redundant projected worked examples were removed while full examples remain in the live policy and score sheets. Responsive checks guard content and navigation from overlap. Actual back-of-room projection still needs a venue rehearsal.

### R18. Central claims depend on sources much of the audience cannot inspect

**Priority: moderate scrutiny limitation. Status: audience-facing summary added; private-source reproducibility remains limited.** Location: slide 24 and [source register](presentation-sources.md#L7).

The contract snapshot and Arrow proposal are access-controlled. Attendees can inspect the public manuscript but may have to trust the presenter on implemented boundaries and planned experimental methods. The source slide says this correctly; disclosure does not remove the practical verification barrier.

**Recommendation:** If the owners approve publication, provide a versioned public mechanism specification or selected excerpts covering score, settlement, appeal funding, identity and trust boundaries. Label any inaccessible claim as source-inspected by the presenter rather than independently reproducible by attendees. Do not publish private code merely to fix this limitation.

**Resolution:** The new [mechanism and evidence page](../public/mechanism/index.html), linked from the deck and handout, describes intended public rules and distinguishes them from the points game, archived paper and access-controlled source inspection. Publication links now reference the verified version 1.2.0 release; later local hardening is labeled unpublished. No private source or uninspected experimental protocol is republished, and the summary does not make the private implementation reproducible. The page becomes remotely available only when this local change is published.

### R19. Participation and retention choices are not explicit at joining

**Priority: moderate. Status: participation and retention notice added.** Locations: slide 12; [join screen](../public/r/index.html#L1177); [standings](../public/r/index.html#L1336); [after-session instructions](facilitator-guide.md#L139).

People enter names, receive publicly attributable results, may receive an instruction to give a false rationale, and are invited to photograph final standings. The reviewed joining copy does not explain these consequences or present observation as a neutral choice. “I do not know enough” frames refusal as a competence judgment. The guide does instruct the host to wipe afterward.

**Recommendation:** Brief participants before joining: aliases are welcome, scores and standings become visible, assigned adversarial role-play may occur, observation is welcome, and specify what is retained or photographed. This is an audience-consent improvement, not a finding that the workshop requires a particular formal approval process.

**Resolution:** Joining and hosting disclose aliases, visible results, assigned roles, observation and optional recusal before play. The guide requires permission for identifiable photos and a verified host wipe after the discussion. It distinguishes deleted session records from retained server-side seat authentication, local credentials/proofs and screenshots. The interface uses neutral sit-out language. No automatic expiry or research consent is fabricated.

## Strengths and criticisms rejected

- Historical NumPy release, integration and migration statements are appropriately bounded. The [official announcement](https://numpy.org/news/#numpy-2-0-0-released), [release notes](https://numpy.org/doc/2.2/release/2.0.0-notes.html), [SciPy notes](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy-1.13.0/release/1.13.0-notes.html) and [migration guide](https://numpy.org/doc/2.0/numpy_2_0_migration_guide.html) support the stated facts. They do not establish illustrative scores, and the deck says so.
- The counterfactual compatibility claim is expressly fictional. It should not be criticized as an allegation NumPy actually made.
- The deck explicitly rejects a general honesty guarantee, a safe collusion threshold, stake as proof of expertise, and proof of public benefit from agreement. These are not newly uncovered omissions.
- The paper, current contract snapshot, Arrow proposal and workshop are materially different. The deck's version discipline is a strength; R01 identifies the live message that undermines it.
- A teaching workshop does not require a randomized trial to be useful. Its observations become insufficient only when promoted into causal or field-validity claims.
- The qualitative public-voice framing is not evidence that this funding system works. The source register generally makes that distinction correctly.
