01 / Why curation matters

Information becomes useful
when it is fit for purpose.

Information quality depends on the user, the task and the context.

Intrinsic

Accuracy · objectivity · credibility

Contextual

Relevance · timeliness · completeness

Representational

Interpretability · consistency

Accessibility

Availability · secure access

Accurate information can still be outdated, irrelevant or unusable.

Adapted from Wang & Strong (1996). Curation assesses these qualities against the requirements of the intended use.

02 / Qualities of information

Information quality
is multidimensional.

Accuracy

Are claims supported by evidence?

Relevance

Do they bear on this decision?

Timeliness

Is the information current enough?

Completeness

Is decision-relevant context missing?

Interpretability

Can the information be understood?

Accessibility

Can intended users obtain and use it?

The purpose determines the quality profile. Assess each dimension separately.

The funding example separates accuracy from relevance; periodic reassessment keeps relevance current.

03 / Trust in curation

Curation creates value.
It also requires trust.

Delegating judgment makes us rely on the curator’s:

Honesty

Sincere reporting of their assessment.

Competence

Ability to understand and evaluate the evidence.

Scrutiny

Critical examination of claims and evidence.

A capable curator still needs to investigate and report honestly.

These are distinct requirements. The mechanism should make all three worthwhile.

04 / Existing approaches

Who curates?
Under which incentives?

A centralized curator

Expertise, context and consistent judgment.

Limited knowledge and capacity.
Concentrated discretion and blind spots.

Simple vote-counting

Broad participation and equal votes.

A tally does not show knowledge,
investigation or strength of evidence.

Can we open participation and make informed judgment worth doing?

Democratic institutions can combine participation with expertise. Open entry alone does not ensure representation.

05 / Game theory

Change the incentives.
Change the outcome.

Game theory asks what participants gain from each strategy.

Reward volume

Encourage more submissions.

Reward agreement

Encourage coordination.

Reward corrections

Encourage investigation.

The game aims to make honesty, competence and scrutiny the most rewarding way to participate.

Outcomes also depend on information, beliefs, costs and expectations. Rewarding agreement alone does not establish accuracy.

06 / A Schelling point

What will everyone
choose?

    Choose the number you expect everyone else to choose.

    Think privately. No discussion.
    On three, say your number together.

    is central, the only even number and a round hundred. Its position, number and gentle tint suggest a shared focal point.

    Can careful assessment of evidence become the focal point of curation?

    Thorough means critical scrutiny. Our shared context makes this strategy a focal point.

    The design aim: make this strategy the most rewarding way to participate.

    A Schelling point coordinates expectations without communication. Salience can guide agreement; it does not establish truth.
